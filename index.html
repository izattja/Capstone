Mountain men were rugged explorers, fur trappers, and traders who roamed the American West roughly between 1810 and the 1840s. While hunting for beaver furs, they charted unknown wilderness, paved the way for westward expansion, and helped shape the early American frontier.

These trappers were driven primarily by the high European demand for beaver pelts to make felt hats, trappers scoured virgin mountain streams. Some worked on salary for large enterprises like the Rocky Mountain Fur Company, while independent "free trappers" sold their pelts to the highest bidder. They faced extreme weather, grizzly bear attacks, starvation, and severe injuries with minimal medical supplies.

Instead of returning to eastern cities each year, trappers gathered at an annual summer rendezvous (such as in the Green River valley of Wyoming) to trade furs for manufactured goods, socialize, and gamble. They lived closely with Native American tribes, frequently adopting their survival methods, clothing, and customs, and often taking native wives. The mountain men came from diverse backgrounds including Anglo-Americans, French-Canadians, Spanish-Americans, and Black frontiersmen such as James Beckwourth and Edward Rose.

By the 1840s, changing European fashions made silk hats more popular than beaver felt, and over-trapping depleted the animal populations, ending the peak fur trade. Many former mountain men used their intimate geographical knowledge of passes and trails to work as military scouts and wagon train guides for settlers heading to Oregon and California.

Here are six of the most famous mountain men:

Jim Bridger

James Felix "Jim" Bridger (1804–1881) was one of the most famous mountain men, explorers, trappers, scouts, and guides of the American West. Born in Richmond, Virginia, he moved to St. Louis as a child and was orphaned at age 13. Although he never learned to read or write, he became fluent in several languages and gained an unmatched knowledge of the Rocky Mountains. At age 18, Bridger joined William Henry Ashley's fur-trapping expedition, beginning a career that spanned nearly five decades. He helped found the Rocky Mountain Fur Company, explored vast areas of the West, and was among the first non-Indigenous Americans to explore Yellowstone's geysers and the Great Salt Lake. In 1843, he and Louis Vasquez established Fort Bridger, an important stop for emigrants traveling the Oregon Trail. Bridger later served as a guide for military expeditions, including the Utah War and Red Cloud's War. He discovered Bridger Pass, which became a key transportation route, and blazed the Bridger Trail to Montana. Although respected for his remarkable wilderness skills and photographic memory, he was also famous for entertaining listeners with colorful tall tales. Married three times to Native American women, he raised five children. Blind in his later years, Bridger died in Missouri in 1881. Today, he is remembered as one of the greatest explorers and pathfinders of the American frontier.

Kit Carson

Christopher Houston "Kit" Carson (1809–1868) was an American frontiersman, fur trapper, wilderness guide, Indian agent, and U.S. Army officer who played a major role in the westward expansion of the United States. Born in Kentucky and raised in Missouri, Carson left home at age 16 to travel the Santa Fe Trail, eventually settling in Taos, New Mexico. He spent more than a decade as a mountain man, trapping furs and exploring the Rocky Mountains alongside renowned frontiersmen such as Jim Bridger. Carson gained national fame in the 1840s as the trusted guide for John C. Frémont's western expeditions, helping map the Oregon Trail, California, and the Great Basin. During the Mexican-American War, he served as a scout and courier, earning a reputation for courage and endurance. Later, Carson served as a federal Indian agent and Union officer during the Civil War. He led campaigns against the Mescalero Apache and Navajo, including the forced removal known as the Long Walk of the Navajo. While once celebrated as a frontier hero, these actions have made his legacy deeply controversial. Carson married three times, had ten children, and remained illiterate throughout his life despite speaking several languages. He died in 1868 in Colorado and was buried in Taos, New Mexico. Today, he is remembered both for his remarkable frontier skills and for his complex, contested role in American history.

James Beckworth

James P. Beckwourth (1798–1866) was a renowned mountain man, fur trapper, explorer, scout, and trader who helped shape the American West. Born into slavery in Virginia to a white planter and an enslaved Black woman, he was freed by his father as a young adult. In 1824, he joined the Rocky Mountain Fur Company and quickly gained a reputation as a skilled trapper and frontiersman. Beckwourth spent nearly a decade living among the Crow people, marrying the daughter of a chief and earning the name "Bull's Robe." According to his account, he rose to become a respected war chief, though historians debate some of these claims. After leaving the Crow, he worked as a trader, Army courier, scout, and guide throughout the West, including during the Mexican–American War. During the California Gold Rush, Beckwourth discovered and improved what became known as Beckwourth Pass and the Beckwourth Trail, a safer, shorter route through the Sierra Nevada that greatly aided emigrants and miners. He later established a ranch, trading post, and hotel in present-day Beckwourth, California.His autobiography, The Life and Adventures of James P. Beckwourth (1856), remains an important source on frontier life, though parts are considered exaggerated. Beckwourth later served as an Army scout during the Colorado and Red Cloud's wars. He died in 1866 while visiting the Crow Nation and was buried among the people who had once adopted him.

Joseph Walker

Joseph Rutherford Walker (1798–1876) was one of the most accomplished explorers, trappers, and trailblazers of the American West. Born in Tennessee, he moved with his family to Missouri, where he became familiar with the Santa Fe Trail and frontier life. In 1832, he joined Benjamin Bonneville's expedition into the Rocky Mountains, launching a career that would make him one of the West's greatest pathfinders. In 1833, Walker led an expedition that discovered a practical overland route to California by following the Humboldt River across present-day Nevada and crossing the Sierra Nevada. This route later became the California Trail, used by thousands of emigrants during the Gold Rush. Although historians debate whether Walker entered Yosemite Valley, he is credited with being among the first Americans to explore the Sierra Nevada and Great Basin extensively. Walker continued exploring throughout the 1840s, leading emigrant wagon trains safely to California and serving as chief guide for John C. Frémont's government expeditions. His extensive knowledge of western geography made him one of the era's most respected guides. In the early 1860s, he led a prospecting expedition into central Arizona that helped spark settlement after gold discoveries near present-day Prescott. Walker spent his final years in California, where he died in 1876. His explorations opened important migration routes and played a major role in the westward expansion of the United States.

John Coulter

John Colter (c. 1774–1812/1813) was one of the earliest and most adventurous explorers of the American West. Born in Virginia and raised on the Kentucky frontier, Colter developed exceptional skills as a hunter, woodsman, and survivalist. In 1803, he joined the Lewis and Clark Expedition, where he became one of the expedition's most trusted scouts and hunters. His knowledge of wilderness travel, ability to communicate with Native peoples, and courage in dangerous situations made him a valuable member of the Corps of Discovery. After the expedition returned in 1806, Colter chose to remain in the wilderness as a fur trapper and mountain man. He explored vast areas of present-day Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho. During his travels in 1807–1808, he became one of the first known European Americans to explore the Yellowstone region, including areas near modern Yellowstone National Park, Jackson Hole, and the Grand Tetons. His reports of geysers, hot springs, and bubbling mud pots were initially dismissed and jokingly called "Colters Hell." Colter became famous for surviving “Colter's Run” in 1809, when he escaped naked from a group of Blackfeet warriors after his companion was killed. He ran miles through hostile territory and survived by hiding in a beaver lodge. After nearly six years in the wilderness, Colter returned to Missouri, served in the War of 1812, and died shortly afterward. His courage and explorations helped open the American West to future travelers and settlers.

Jedediah Smith

Jedediah Strong Smith (1799–1831) was one of the most important explorers and mountain men of the early American West. Born in New York and raised in a modest family, Smith developed a love for exploration and wilderness travel. In 1822, he joined William H. Ashley's fur trading company and quickly gained a reputation for courage, leadership, and survival skills. Smith led several groundbreaking expeditions that greatly expanded American knowledge of the West. In 1824, he helped identify South Pass, a practical route through the Rocky Mountains that later became the main pathway for emigrants traveling the Oregon Trail. He and his companions were the first U.S. citizens to cross the Mojave Desert, explore parts of California, cross the Sierra Nevada, and travel overland from California into the Oregon Country. Throughout his career, Smith survived numerous dangers, including a severe grizzly bear attack, hostile encounters with Native groups, and harsh desert conditions. He was also an excellent cartographer whose maps helped guide later explorers, including John C. Frémont. In 1831, Smith attempted to organize a government-sponsored western expedition but was unsuccessful. Soon afterward, while leading a trading expedition along the Santa Fe Trail, he disappeared and was killed by Comanche warriors in present-day Kansas. Although largely forgotten after his death, Smith was later recognized as one of America's greatest explorers, whose discoveries helped open the West to future settlement.
